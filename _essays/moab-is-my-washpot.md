Stephen Fry borrowed the phrase Moab Is My Washpot from the Psalms to title his first autobiography — a book that traces his early years with honesty, wit, and more than a pinch of self-awareness. I borrow it here for much the same reason. If Fry’s "Moab" was a boys' boarding school in England, mine was a girls' Catholic convent school in India — a place that raised me for 14 years until I emerged equal parts inhibited and empowered. 

A convent education is a strange thing to look back on. On one hand, it’s all rules, restrictions, and nuns who could probably hear you chewing gum from two classrooms away. On the other hand, it was a cocoon — a space where I discovered myself before the world could tell me who I should be. Our upbringing was strict, no doubt about that, but the school was also a factory of fun, laughter, and a quiet sort of rebellion tucked between the lines of its rulebook.

There’s something oddly liberating about growing up in an all-girls school. There were no boys to impress, no pressure to conform to anyone else’s standards of pretty or polite. We ran, played, shouted, and sweated through sports and physical activities with zero self-consciousness. Puberty came and went in the company of people who were all going through the same storm — there was strength in that shared awkwardness.

That’s not to say the school didn’t try to tame us. It did — with military precision. Appearance was tightly regulated. Lip color? Absolutely not. Hair dye? Banned. Skirt length? Below the knee, no exceptions. Hair clips? No more than three, and all black. Even hair bands had to be black — God forbid a splash of pink should derail the moral compass of the campus. Of course, the nuns made sure we knew the world outside was a minefield. Talking to boys who weren’t your brothers or relatives was an automatic black mark against your soul. Dragging your shoes while walking was grotesque. Sitting with your legs crossed in front of an elder was scandalous. Talking with folded hands across your chest was rude. Eating with your mouth open was unpardonable. And my personal favourite: your back must always be straight. We learned etiquette like our lives depended on it. Non-convent types, I’ve often joked, probably wouldn’t know how to spell “etiquette,” much less put it into practice.

Now, to some, this might sound oppressive. Maybe it was. But deep down, I still believe school is an institution where one must learn mannerisms. Better to learn how to eat with your mouth closed at ten than to traumatise innocent diners at twenty.

I still carry some values, or perhaps more accurately, deeply instilled fears from my school days — fears that have somehow clung to me like a second skin. One of the most persistent ones is the fear of mispronouncing a word. It’s like a shadow that follows me every time I open my mouth. Even now, I find myself double-checking the pronunciation of a word in my head before I say it out loud, as if someone, somewhere, is secretly waiting for me to slip up and make a fool of myself.

At our school, it didn’t matter what subject you were in — every teacher, regardless of their field, seemed to believe they were a grammar expert first and foremost. God forbid you ever mistakenly asked for a “scissor” in the art class. The teacher would immediately pounce, taking the opportunity to lecture the class on how it must be “scissors,” not “scissor.” We’d sometimes be left staring, bewildered, as discussions that were supposed to focus solely on the subject matter at hand would suddenly derail into full-blown grammar lessons. A conversation about art supplies would end up being an impromptu English lecture on plural forms, and more often than not, we were caught in the crossfire. It was both hilarious and mildly terrifying, knowing that language itself could sneak into the most unexpected corners of our day.

Thinking of more memories from school, I remember one particularly glorious day when I was in 10th standard. Our class teacher, a woman of unfathomable moods who taught mathematics with the intensity of a drill sergeant leading a battlefield charge, was stalking around the room, barking formulas at us. With her, you never knew what might trigger a nuclear meltdown. It could be a wrong answer, a yawn, a slightly slanted notebook — or, as we learned that day, the shocking revelation that some of us were bra-less under our crisp, innocent white shirts.

"Class monitor!" she barked, her voice slicing through the air like a whip. The poor girl leapt to her feet, looking like she’d been called to the guillotine. "Inspect," the teacher ordered, with the gravity of someone demanding a border security sweep. "Each one. NOW."

It was a massacre. More than half the class was exposed — not physically, thank goodness, but morally, in the eyes of our scandalized math teacher. "From tomorrow onwards," she declared, "if I find anyone without a BRA," she glared at us from above her thick glasses, "you will each run five rounds of the field. Under the midday sun. In front of the entire school. Your sore breasts will make sure you remember to wear a bra next time."

I loved the school campus. Not for any superficial reasons like the fancy facilities it offered — in fact, it was one of the simplest, even outdated, compared to other modern schools. What drew me in was its essence, the way it held the history of countless individuals who had walked those same grounds, spent their formative years within its walls, and left behind echoes of their stories.

The library was my personal Narnia. Tucked away in a quiet corner of the school, it was my favourite place to spend hot afternoons. The shelves, crammed with books of every shape and size, felt like a labyrinth that invited exploration and discovery. Hidden in those dusty, overstuffed shelves were entire worlds — The Hobbit, Britannica, Gray’s Anatomy (the book, not the soap opera). I would often choose a random book, sit on the floor next to one of the shelves and let myself get lost in it. Surprisingly, the library even had medical texts as the school shared campus with a charitable hospital also run by the same convent. 

Each year at school felt more intense and demanding than the previous. The lessons grew tougher, the expectations higher, and the reality of life outside the school gates started to settle in.

The farewell day arrived with mixed emotions, a bittersweet sense of finality hanging in the air. All of us were first taken to the chapel to pray, where the nuns had some heart warming last messages for us. Next we were asked to move to the auditorium with some mysterious event planned. The atmosphere in the auditorium was charged with nervous excitement, the buzz of whispers and shifting seats creating an almost theatrical tension. The school principal, a stern but kind-hearted woman, stood at the front with an air of authority. She was ready with a projector and a slide show, a sure sign that this would be no ordinary farewell address.

“Today, we are going to discuss a very important subject that concerns your future,” she began, her voice echoing across the auditorium. The room fell into a hushed silence as the projector whirred to life behind her, the first slide flicking onto the screen. "Abortion," she said, with an intensity that could almost make you believe we were about to have a moral reckoning. "It is a sin against the will of God."

The room grew quieter still, as we exchanged confused glances. This was the last thing we expected to discuss on our farewell day. The principal's voice grew more solemn, her gaze scanning the room of young faces. “A child is a blessing, girls. Always remember that. A child is a gift, a precious soul, and no matter the circumstances, you must always accept a child with open arms." I glanced around at the girls sitting beside me. Some were wide-eyed, fidgeting uncomfortably. Others giggling, amused by the subject.

The principal, apparently not noticing our discomfort, continued her sermon with passion. “You girls are the future of this country. You must uphold the values of motherhood and family. Never let society tell you that it's okay to discard a life." Finally, the slide show ended, and the principal turned off the projector, clasping her hands in front of her with a satisfied nod. And so, this is how our time at school ended — with the same absurdity that had marked our journey all along.

My school was a place that scrubbed, scoured, and polished me into someone stronger, someone scarred, someone funny enough to survive the madness. I walked out carrying rules I swore I’d rebel against, but also the kind of steel you don’t even realize you’ve forged until years later. And honestly? I wouldn’t have it any other way.
