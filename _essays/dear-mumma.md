September has returned, heavy with rain, and that brings me back to you. That reminds me of our last stretch together, the relentless downpour followed by a long winter.

Of course, we are not just the sum of those final days we shared. We spent twenty-two years together. Twenty-two years that felt like sixty in their richness and not the dull kind, but the kind lived with attention. I flaunt this thoughtful disposition I’ve inherited from you, the way others flaunt their generational wealth.

I live in Bangalore now. K and I moved here soon after her 10th board exams were over. You’d be glad to know she finished her last two years of school in Bangalore and has now joined a college in Kannur, Kerala. She’s pursuing the fanciest degree anyone in our family has ever had the chance to attain. As usual, Papa is more excited about K’s higher education than she is herself. You might find it hard to believe, but he actually insisted she study in South India. He has fallen in love with Bangalore and the South as a whole. You already know about his love for travelling. Now the two of us are just helping unlock more places for him to explore.

You might wonder what it’s like to live in Bangalore. If I had to sum it up in a single word, it would be diversity. People from every corner of the country are here. Bihar, Chandigarh, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and even places you may never have heard of. It’s vibrant and full of variety, and I rarely feel homesick for Kanpur. In fact, I sometimes think I enjoy Bangalore more than most people here, simply because I had lived my entire life in Kanpur before moving. And though Bangalore is strikingly different, every now and then something happens that pulls me back to Kanpur and its peculiar quirks.

Bengaluru is infamous for its poor traffic management. To make it worse, the roads leading to my office are in terrible condition, with potholes the size of craters on the moon. You'd look at some and wonder if an asteroid had struck the Earth at that very spot. One evening, on my way back from the office, I got stuck in traffic. I was minding my own business, checking my phone, replying to work messages, when I suddenly heard a man yelling. A Kannadiga(natives of Karnataka are called that) was hurling abuse at a Sardar. At this point, I don’t think I have to make it more interesting for you. Two men from different cultures clashing in public is drama enough.

The complaint? The Sardar’s bike silencer was touching his tire. Not moving, not colliding, just resting there while the traffic stood frozen. Yet the Kannadiga was fuming as though someone had insulted his ancestors. Naturally, the Sardar wasn’t going to take it quietly. He snapped back with a full range of Hindi curse words. The kind men use to bait each other into fights. Another flare of male rage, I thought to myself. It is such a widely accepted trait of men. Society dresses it up as passion, as strength, and even desirability. I’ll tell you about what went down between Elon Musk and Donald Trump sometime. That’ll be an interesting one.

Do you remember how opinionated and stubborn I was when you last saw me? Well, I’ve only grown worse since then. My opinions now stretch across politics, religion, business, and technology, layered on top of the old favorites you knew so well, education, people, feminism. And honestly, carrying this many opinions is exhausting. Every day I try to look past people’s prejudices and sheer nonsense, but more often than not, that’s all there is to see.

Back in college, you might remember, I was glued to my laptop for hours. You wouldn’t be too pleased to hear it’s only gotten worse. Now I carry two laptops, work and personal, and both feel surgically attached to me, like extra organs I can’t live without. Work demands it of course, my current team has long and hectic on-call hours, the kind where I can be woken up from sleep to fix issues. And in the past few months, I’ve buried myself in hyper-aggressive projects, voluntarily, because I seem to thrive on challenges and I’ve earned a reputation for the same in my team. And when I’m not working? I’m still in front of the screen by choice, wandering aimlessly through the internet. Because as much as it’s unhealthy, surfing online is still addictive fun. On top of that, for an introvert like me, the virtual company of clever characters in books and movies feels far more comforting than the effort of meeting people in the flesh. You used to nag me to take breaks from the screen, but here no one bothers. Everyone seems perfectly content in front of their own screens.

Thankfully, a sliver of my life does exist outside the internet bubble. Almost every other weekend I head into central Bangalore, where the city finally looks like the sophisticated metropolitan it claims to be. The old architecture and design make it clear this isn’t some freshly built corporate hub but a place with history. Getting there takes about an hour to cover just 15 kilometers, a shocking distance by Kanpur standards, but here it’s routine. The first five kilometers or so while passing all the tech parks are horrible, bad roads and traffic. Then suddenly, the road exhales into green. The trees arch overhead, their branches clasping like hands. Sometimes I don’t even want to reach a destination, I just want to keep driving under that canopy. It’s one of the few things that can pull a smile out of me, which you know is no small feat. Maybe the city feels mundane to others, but to me, reaching its beauty after slogging through chaos all week makes it all the more precious. Light always seems brighter after the dark.

You would love it here, just like I do.

Thankfully, a tiny part of my life does exist outside the internet bubble. Almost every other weekend, I go to central Bangalore, that’s where the proper development of a metropolitan can be seen. From the design and buildings, one can also tell that it is very old, not some newly built corporate city. To reach the city, I have to drive for about an hour, a distance close to 15 km. While that would be a lot in Kanpur, this distance is very typical, required to go from one place to another. For the first five kilometers, the roads and traffic are horrible. Then suddenly, the road exhales into green. The trees arch overhead, their branches clasping like hands, and for a while it feels like driving inside a cathedral. Through the canopy of the thick trees lining both sides of the road. Sometimes, I just want to drive around and not stop at any particular destination. It puts a big smile on my face. You know how difficult it is to put a smile on this face. The city might be mundane and boring for others. Does it make it extra pleasurable to reach the nicer parts of the city after going through all the traffic? I don’t have an answer. The light does seem special after being in the dark.

There’s much more, Mumma, as you can imagine, so much more I need to tell you about. I could fill hundreds of pages with all that has unfolded in the lives of your three musketeers since you left. There are so many stories I know would have had you doubled over, laughing until you were out of breath. I don’t think I ever mentioned this, but I used to love to make you laugh. I’d collect the day’s absurdities, then retell them with all the theatrics I could muster just to get that laugh out of you.

I’ll pause here for now, only to return soon. If not with words, then in my dreams because you’re always there, as if you never left.
